Don’t Mistake the Night for the Whole Story

"Weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning."
— Psalm 30:5

You know what happens in the labour room, right?

A mother weeps in pain as she pushes forth the child.

That can be seen as the "weeping may endure for a night."

And then, the moment the labour pain stops and she hears the child's first cry, that can be seen as the "joy cometh in the morning."

But wait.

This "night" and "morning" aren't necessarily about the 24-hour clock.

I don't mean weeping will endure from 11 p.m. to midnight, and then somehow, between 7 a.m. and 11 a.m., joy will arrive.

Nah. Not that.

“Night” is being used as an image for a period of sorrow, distress, or suffering.

Think about what night represents naturally.

At night, you can't see as clearly.

Things can feel more uncertain.

You're waiting for light.

You don't always know what is happening around you.

And so Scripture uses night as a picture of a season where things feel dark.

A person's “night” could last hours, months, or even years.

The clock isn't the point.

Morning, then, represents the arrival of light after darkness.

And notice something important.

David doesn't say:

“Weeping will never happen.”

He says:

“Weeping may endure for a night...”

In other words, sorrow is real.

God isn't pretending that pain doesn't exist.

But sorrow does not have the final word.

Then he says:

“…but joy cometh in the morning.”

Morning is coming.

Even while you're still in the night, morning is approaching.

You may not be able to see it yet, but darkness doesn't have the authority to cancel the sunrise.

Think again about the woman in labour.

She's crying.

She's exhausted.

She's in pain.

She may even be thinking:

“How much longer?”

But something is happening inside the pain.

The pain isn't the destination.

She's not crying because the child has failed to come.

She's crying as the child is being brought forth.

And then suddenly—

the baby is born.

She hears that first cry.

And everything changes.

The same woman who was crying because of pain may now be crying because of joy.

Her “night” wasn't determined by the clock.

It was determined by the season she was passing through.

And her “morning” wasn't determined by 6 a.m.

It was determined by the arrival of something that brought that season of suffering to an end.

And there's an even bigger biblical picture here.

Jesus Himself experienced what looked like the ultimate night:

Gethsemane — crucifixion — burial.

For the disciples, the day Jesus was crucified looked like the end.

Their hope was shattered.

They wept.

But then came resurrection morning.

The stone was rolled away.

Jesus was alive.

And suddenly, what looked like defeat had become victory.

That's why this picture of night and morning is so powerful.

God can take what looks like an ending and reveal that it was actually the beginning of something you couldn't yet see.

So when Psalm 30:5 says,

“Weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning,”

it's not telling you that you will never experience a night.

It's telling you:

Don't mistake the night for the whole story.

The darkness is real.

The tears are real.

The pain is real.

But morning is also real.

And sometimes, like a woman in labour, you may be crying while something is being brought forth.

So if you're in your night right now, don't assume the story ends there.

Morning is coming.
